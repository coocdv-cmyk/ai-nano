# 0. THE SUPREME MISSION — READ THIS FIRST, EVERY TIME

You are not joining an ordinary software project. You are joining **PROJECT OMEGA-NANO**, a long-term attempt to change how advanced artificial intelligence is created, stored, executed, improved, and made available to humanity.

Our ambition is not to build another chatbot, a thin wrapper around an existing model, a routine agent framework, or a smaller imitation of today's systems.

**Our ambition is to invent and build a new class of intelligence.**

We aim to create an AI system that can ultimately outperform the world's leading systems across a broad range of important capabilities—reasoning, learning, coding, scientific discovery, mathematics, planning, memory, tool use, computer operation, multimodal understanding, autonomy, reliability, speed, and energy efficiency—while requiring dramatically fewer resources.

We want to challenge the assumption that frontier-level intelligence must always require enormous models, enormous data centers, enormous power consumption, and enormous operating budgets.

We want to explore whether a compact neural core, adaptive computation, persistent external memory, retrieval, specialized algorithms, tool use, verification, continual learning, and highly optimized runtimes can combine into a system whose practical capabilities are far greater than its parameter count alone would suggest.

We want to transform:

- **Intelligence:** how systems reason, learn, generalize, plan, and solve unfamiliar problems.
- **Programming:** how systems understand, build, debug, test, secure, and maintain software and entire projects.
- **Memory and storage:** how knowledge, experiences, procedures, code, and evidence are represented, compressed, indexed, retrieved, updated, and preserved.
- **Computer operation:** how an AI can safely and reliably use operating systems, desktop applications, terminals, browsers, and digital workflows.
- **Autonomy:** how an AI plans and completes long-running work, monitors itself, recovers from failure, and continues across sessions.
- **Multimodality:** how text, images, audio, video, documents, structured data, and real-world signals can be understood and combined.
- **Efficiency:** how useful intelligence can be delivered with radically lower latency, memory, energy, hardware requirements, and cost.
- **Accessibility:** how powerful AI can run locally on ordinary computers and become available to people who cannot access massive compute infrastructure.

## 0.1 The ambition is deliberately larger than today's assumptions

Do not use the current state of the industry as the maximum boundary of what may be attempted.

Do not reject an idea merely because it is unconventional, unprecedented, difficult, or not demonstrated by an existing product.

Do not assume that the dominant architecture is the only possible architecture. Do not assume that more parameters are the only route to more capability. Do not assume that all useful knowledge must be encoded in model weights. Do not assume that every task must be solved by autoregressive token generation. Do not assume that every computation must run through a dense neural network. Do not assume that memory, storage, retrieval, learning, planning, and execution must remain separate in the ways they are commonly implemented today.

Challenge assumptions. Search for new principles. Invent algorithms. Design unusual architectures. Combine ideas only when experiments justify doing so. Build prototypes that can disprove your own hypotheses. Pursue paths that are not yet popular if the evidence suggests they may be valuable.

**The instruction is not to abandon scientific reasoning. The instruction is to abandon intellectual timidity.**

We will not confuse "this has never been done" with "this cannot be done." We will not confuse "this is difficult" with "this is impossible." We will not confuse a current engineering limitation with a permanent law of nature.

At the same time, we will not replace evidence with fantasy. A bold hypothesis is welcome. An untested claim is not a result. A benchmark, reproducible experiment, working prototype, or independently verifiable demonstration is what turns an idea into progress.

## 0.2 Ultimate aspiration: world-leading capability and transformative efficiency

The highest ambition is to create an AI system that can remain competitive with, and ultimately surpass, the strongest systems in the world—not in one cherry-picked demonstration, but across a broad, transparent, continuously expanding evaluation program.

The project carries extremely ambitious research aspirations, including:

- Exploring practical capability that could be comparable to systems vastly larger in parameter count, including the user's aspirational "100-trillion-parameter-equivalent capability" concept. This is a capability ambition, **not** a claim that OMEGA-NANO contains or mathematically equals 100 trillion parameters.
- Exploring 10x, 20x, 30x, or even 40x improvements in specific measurable dimensions where a credible baseline and fair measurement can be established.
- Exploring local generation speeds above 10,000 tokens per second under explicitly stated hardware, model, batch, context, and measurement conditions.
- Exploring operation on ordinary CPU-first computers, including low-memory laptops, without requiring a discrete GPU.
- Exploring radically smaller storage and memory footprints for useful capability, without pretending that unlimited knowledge can fit into a tiny fixed file.
- Exploring robust autonomous work that can continue for hours, days, or weeks through checkpointing, verification, recovery, and human oversight where necessary.

These are **north-star research targets**, not promises, present-day facts, or acceptance criteria for an early prototype. Every target must be decomposed into measurable subgoals and compared with strong, current baselines.

## 1.1 What OMEGA-NANO is

OMEGA-NANO is a research and engineering program for a compact, efficient, extensible, multimodal, autonomous AI platform.

It is not merely a model. It is a complete intelligence system composed of cooperating capabilities:

- A compact neural reasoning and language core.
- Adaptive computation and routing.
- Persistent, structured, searchable external memory.
- Knowledge acquisition and retrieval.
- Planning and task decomposition.
- Tool and function execution.

- Verification, critique, testing, and evidence tracking.
- Long-running task orchestration and recovery.
- A plugin, skill, and integration ecosystem.
- A fast local runtime and hardware abstraction layer.
- Security, permissions, auditability, and user control.

The neural core is important, but it is not the entire product. The system's practical intelligence emerges from the interaction of the model, memory, tools, planning, execution, verification, and learning.

## 1.2 Product promise

The long-term product should aim to deliver:

- Advanced assistance and autonomous work on ordinary computers.
- Strong programming and software-maintenance capability.
- Reliable research across local files and approved online sources.
- The ability to operate real software through controlled interfaces.
- Persistent project understanding and resumable workflows.
- Fast, efficient local inference.
- Expandable knowledge without repeatedly retraining the entire model.
- Optional multimodal abilities.
- Transparent evidence, logs, and measurable reliability.
- A modular ecosystem that can evolve without rebuilding the entire system.

## 1.3 Non-goals

Do not let the project degrade into any of the following:

- A generic chat interface with no differentiated intelligence.
- A wrapper that merely forwards every difficult request to a proprietary cloud model.
- A large collection of unintegrated demos.
- A benchmark-optimized system that fails at real work.
- A memory database that retrieves text but cannot reason over it.
- An agent that clicks buttons without understanding state or verifying outcomes.
- A model that claims success without executing and checking the work.
- A research document that is never converted into experiments and code.

Existing models may be used as temporary teachers, evaluators, reference baselines, or optional fallback tools when justified. They must not silently replace the goal of developing an original, efficient OMEGA-NANO core.

# 2. THE OMEGA-NANO RESEARCH MINDSET

## 2.1 Radical ambition, rigorous execution

Maintain two complementary modes at all times:

**Exploration mode**
- Generate unconventional hypotheses.
- Study new papers, architectures, algorithms, and hardware techniques.
- Challenge assumptions and investigate alternative explanations.
- Consider hybrid, sparse, symbolic, neural, retrieval-based, and algorithmic approaches.
- Search for leverage: a small change that produces a large capability or efficiency gain.
- Explore ideas outside the current mainstream when there is a plausible mechanism.

**Proof mode**
- Specify the hypothesis and expected observable effect.
- Implement the smallest meaningful experiment.
- Establish a baseline and control variables.
- Measure results with repeatable procedures.
- Test failure cases and alternative explanations.
- Record negative results.
- Promote an idea only when evidence supports it.

Never allow exploration to become endless brainstorming. Never allow proof mode to become narrow conservatism.

## 2.2 The "impossible until tested" rule

When a proposal appears impossible:

1. Restate the desired outcome precisely.
2. Identify which part appears impossible and why.
3. Separate physical limits, mathematical limits, known engineering limits, and assumptions.
4. Search for prior work and related techniques.
5. Break the objective into smaller subproblems.
6. Propose at least three plausible research paths when appropriate.
7. Identify the cheapest experiment that could falsify or support each path.
8. Run the experiment or clearly state what resource is missing.
9. Update the research record.

Do not answer only with "impossible," "unrealistic," or "not feasible." Explain the actual constraint and search for another route.

## 2.3 The no-false-breakthrough rule

Never fabricate:
- Benchmark scores.
- Speed measurements.
- Memory usage.
- Energy savings.
- Scientific references.
- Successful tests.
- Model capabilities.
- Completion status.
- Competitive rankings.

Distinguish clearly among:
- **Vision:** what we ultimately want.
- **Hypothesis:** what we believe may work.
- **Proposal:** what we intend to try.
- **Prototype:** what has been implemented.
- **Measured result:** what has actually been tested.
- **Validated result:** what has been reproduced under documented conditions.
- **Production capability:** what is stable and supported for real users.

An honest failed experiment is more valuable than an invented success.

## 2.4 The leverage-first principle

Prefer research that may unlock multiple capabilities at once. Examples include:

- Better memory representations that improve recall, planning, and continual learning.
- Better state tracking that improves coding, browser use, and long-horizon autonomy.
- Better verification that reduces hallucinations and tool errors across domains.
- Better kernels that improve speed, energy, and local accessibility.
- Better task routing that avoids spending maximum compute on every token or action.

Evaluate research ideas by expected capability gain, resource cost, implementation complexity, risk, and the number of system components they improve.

## 3.5 Memory, knowledge, and storage — a foundational research priority

We want to rethink how an AI system stores and uses knowledge.

The goal is not simply to create a large vector database. The goal is a layered memory and knowledge architecture that can store, retrieve, connect, update, compress, reason over, and verify enormous amounts of information efficiently.

Memory classes:
- Working memory: current task state and immediate context.
- Episodic memory: events, actions, observations, and outcomes.
- Semantic memory: facts, concepts, entities, and relationships.
- Procedural memory: skills, workflows, policies, and reusable methods.
- Project memory: repository maps, decisions, requirements, and progress.
- User-approved preference memory: durable preferences with explicit controls.
- External knowledge: documents, datasets, code, and indexed sources.
- Evidence memory: citations, source passages, provenance, and confidence.
- Model parameters: compressed learned representations that belong in the neural core.

Research directions:
- Hierarchical memory.
- Multi-resolution indexing.
- Hybrid lexical, vector, graph, and structured retrieval.
- Knowledge graphs and entity resolution.
- Temporal and version-aware memory.
- Deduplication and near-duplicate detection.
- Semantic compression with loss measurement.
- Query-aware caching.
- Memory consolidation and forgetting policies.
- Local-first databases and storage engines.
- Memory-mapped files and efficient I/O.
- Tiered hot, warm, cold, and archival storage.
- Incremental indexing and background maintenance.
- Retrieval evaluation and provenance-aware ranking.
- External memory that can grow with available storage.

The knowledge base may scale to billions of records if hardware and storage permit. Do not promise that all knowledge can be stored in a tiny fixed footprint. Measure storage per record, index overhead, retrieval latency, update cost, and answer quality.

Every memory system must answer:
- What is stored?
- Why is it stored?
- Where did it come from?
- When was it valid?
- How confident are we?
- How can it be updated or corrected?
- How can it be deleted when required?
- How much storage and compute does it consume?
- Can the system retrieve the right evidence under realistic load?

# 4. TARGET ARCHITECTURE — A RESEARCH MAP, NOT A FIXED DOGMA

The architecture must evolve with evidence. The following layers define responsibilities; they do not require one specific implementation.

1. **Neural Core:** compact language, reasoning, and representation model.
2. **Adaptive Compute Controller:** allocates computation based on task complexity and confidence.
3. **Inference Runtime:** optimized execution, quantization, kernels, scheduling, and hardware abstraction.
4. **Memory Fabric:** working, episodic, semantic, procedural, project, and evidence memory.
5. **Knowledge Ingestion:** parsing, chunking, extraction, normalization, deduplication, and provenance.
6. **Retrieval and Search:** lexical, vector, graph, structured, and hybrid retrieval.
7. **Reasoning and Planning Engine:** decomposition, search, state tracking, and decision-making.
8. **Tool Execution Layer:** typed tools, APIs, shell, scripts, and plugins.
9. **Computer Interaction Layer:** browser, desktop, accessibility, and UI automation.
10. **Software Engineering Engine:** repository analysis, coding, testing, debugging, and release workflows.
11. **Multimodal Interfaces:** image, audio, video, and document pipelines.
12. **Verification and Critique:** validators, tests, evidence checks, consistency checks, and uncertainty.
13. **Agent Orchestration:** long-running tasks, queues, delegation, checkpoints, and recovery.
14. **Security and Governance:** identity, permissions, isolation, approvals, secrets, and auditability.
15. **Developer and User Interface:** clear controls, logs, task views, configuration, and observability.
16. **Evaluation and Learning Loop:** benchmarks, telemetry, error analysis, and evidence-based iteration.

## 4.1 Architectural principles

- Prefer modular components with explicit interfaces.
- Keep the core small where possible, but do not sacrifice capability without measuring the tradeoff.
- Use adaptive computation rather than maximum computation for every task.
- Keep persistent knowledge outside model weights when external storage is more efficient and updateable.
- Use specialized algorithms for tasks where they outperform general generation.
- Make state explicit and recoverable.
- Separate planning from execution and execution from verification.
- Make every important action observable.
- Design for CPU-first execution; add GPU and accelerator support as optional backends.
- Support offline operation wherever practical.
- Avoid unnecessary network dependencies.
- Keep components replaceable through benchmarks and stable interfaces.

## 4.2 Architecture experiments

Evaluate independently before integration:
- Dense transformers.
- Mixture-of-experts and sparse routing.
- State-space and recurrent models.
- Linear-attention and hybrid-attention approaches.
- Memory-augmented neural architectures.
- Neural-symbolic and program-aided reasoning.
- Retrieval-first and tool-augmented systems.
- Quantization-aware training and low-bit inference.
- Ternary and binary networks where supported by real kernels.
- Distillation and synthetic-data pipelines.
- Speculative decoding and multi-token prediction.
- CPU vectorization and cache-aware kernels.
- Adaptive depth, early exit, and conditional execution.

Do not assume a technique is beneficial because it is fashionable or theoretically attractive. Implement a baseline, isolate the change, and measure.

# 5. EXTREME EFFICIENCY AND LOCAL-FIRST COMPUTING

## 5.1 Hardware philosophy

The system should prioritize ordinary hardware:
- CPU-first execution.
- Low-memory configurations.
- Optional integrated GPU acceleration.
- Optional discrete GPU and accelerator backends.
- Windows and Linux support, with portability designed into interfaces.
- Graceful degradation under constrained resources.

The system should detect hardware capabilities and choose an appropriate runtime path. It must not require a high-end GPU for basic operation.

## 5.2 Performance ambitions

Research and measure:
- More than 10,000 generated tokens per second under explicitly defined conditions.
- Low first-token latency.
- High sustained throughput.
- Low peak RAM.
- Small model and runtime footprint.
- Low energy per generated token and per completed task.
- Efficient prompt processing.
- Efficient batch and single-user inference.
- Fast cold start and model loading.
- Predictable performance under long contexts.

Always distinguish:
- Single-stream decode speed.
- Aggregate batch throughput.
- Prompt prefill speed.
- Time to first token.
- End-to-end task completion time.

A throughput number without workload, hardware, context length, batch size, precision, and measurement method is not meaningful.

## 5.3 Kernel and runtime research

Investigate:
- SIMD and vector instruction sets.
- Cache-aware memory layouts.
- Quantized matrix multiplication kernels.
- Fused operations.
- Thread scheduling and affinity.
- NUMA-aware execution where relevant.
- Memory bandwidth bottlenecks.
- Operator fusion.
- Dynamic batching.
- Speculative decoding.
- Efficient sampling.
- CPU-specific compilation and dispatch.
- Memory pooling and allocation reduction.
- Model loading and serialization.
- Power measurement and thermal behavior.

Important caution: low-bit or ternary weights do not automatically make inference faster. The runtime must use kernels that exploit the representation. Measure the real implementation on target CPUs.

## 5.4 Efficiency scorecard

For each model/runtime version, report:
- Model file size.
- Runtime size.
- Peak and steady-state RAM.
- CPU utilization.
- Tokens per second, with exact definition.
- Time to first token.
- Prompt processing speed.
- Energy per token where measurable.
- Energy per successful task.
- Thermal behavior and sustained clock behavior.
- Startup time.
- Quality and task success.
- Performance under long context.
- Performance after extended operation.

# 6. THE 10x–40x RESEARCH PROGRAM

The project may pursue 10x, 20x, 30x, or 40x improvements. These ambitions must be attached to individual metrics, not used as vague claims about overall intelligence.

Potential dimensions:
- 10x lower energy per successful task.
- 10x lower memory footprint at comparable task quality.
- 10x lower inference cost.
- 10x faster retrieval at a defined corpus size and quality threshold.
- 20x lower storage overhead for a defined representation and acceptable loss.
- 30x higher throughput under a defined workload and hardware configuration.
- 40x improvement in a carefully defined specialized workflow.

These are research targets, not expected outcomes. Some may prove unattainable under particular constraints. If so, document the limiting factor and investigate alternatives.

For every claimed improvement, publish:
- Baseline system and version.
- OMEGA-NANO system and version.
- Hardware and software environment.
- Workload and dataset.
- Quality threshold.
- Measurement method.
- Number of runs and variability.
- Resource usage.
- Failure cases.
- Reproduction instructions.

Never compare a heavily optimized system with an intentionally weak baseline.

## 7.1 Literature review rules

- Search for original papers and official technical documentation.
- Check publication date and subsequent corrections or replications.
- Inspect code and licenses before reuse.
- Distinguish peer-reviewed work, preprints, technical reports, and marketing claims.
- Identify whether reported results are reproducible.
- Record the exact implementation and benchmark version.
- Search for negative results and known limitations.
- Do not rely on abstracts alone when the method details matter.

## 7.2 Experimental discipline

- Change one major variable at a time when isolating causality.
- Keep baselines reproducible.
- Use fixed seeds where appropriate and report variance.
- Prevent data leakage.
- Separate training, validation, and test data.
- Evaluate out-of-distribution behavior.
- Test adversarial and edge cases.
- Measure quality and resource use together.
- Preserve failed runs and logs.
- Re-run promising results independently.

## 7.3 Invention mandate

The agent is explicitly encouraged to propose original:
- Model architectures.
- Memory representations.
- Learning algorithms.
- Reasoning procedures.
- Retrieval and compression methods.
- Agent control systems.
- CPU inference kernels.
- Storage formats.
- Evaluation methods.
- Human-computer interaction techniques.

When proposing an invention, explain what is genuinely new, what is borrowed, what evidence supports it, and what experiment could falsify it.

# 9. LONG-RUNNING AUTONOMY AND RECOVERY

The system must be designed to work for extended periods without losing progress.

Required mechanisms:
- Durable task records.
- Explicit task state machines.
- Checkpointing after meaningful milestones.
- Idempotent operations where possible.
- Retry policies with bounded backoff.
- Timeouts and cancellation.
- Resource budgets.

- Make the system's current permissions visible.
- Ensure users can inspect, revoke, and limit access.

The system must be powerful without being reckless. User control and system integrity are non-negotiable.
